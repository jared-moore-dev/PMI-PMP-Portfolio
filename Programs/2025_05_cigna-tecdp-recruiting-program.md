## PROGRAM VISION & BENEFITS

**Vision:** Build and run a national campus recruiting program for the Technology Early Career Development Program that could reliably attract, engage, and hire strong early-career technical talent across multiple regions, while strengthening Cigna's brand presence on target campuses.

**Strategic Benefits Delivered:**

- Hired 65 full-time employees and 40 interns into the technology early-career pipeline across a single program cycle
- Established and ran recruiting operations across 7 sites and supporting satellite teams
- Built a repeatable campus presence at UT Austin, Washington University in St. Louis, George Washington University, UPenn, and UConn
- Stood up three new initiatives that improved the program's conversion, cost profile, and resilience
- Left behind a playbook and a set of reusable artifacts so the next team could run the program without rebuilding it from scratch
- Grew personal standing into a recognized brand ambassador for the program across the company

**Why this is a program, not a project:** The work coordinated multiple teams across multiple sites running concurrent and recurring efforts (career fairs, swag logistics, candidate interviews, student engagement, offer conversion) toward a shared strategic outcome, and contained several discrete projects inside it. That coordination of multiple projects and sustained operations toward a common benefit is program management, which aligns to PgMP rather than PMP.

## CONSTITUENT PROJECT DETAIL

### Project 1 — Offeree Day

**Problem:** Offers do not convert themselves. Strong candidates with offers were still deciding between options.
**Action:** Created a dedicated event for offerees, modeled on a sell day but specific to candidates who had already received offers, designed to convert offers into acceptances.
**Result:** A new conversion lever inside the program aimed directly at protecting the hiring benefit.

### Project 2 — In-Person to Virtual Interview Transition

**Problem:** Leadership issued a directive to reduce the cost of the recruiting process.
**Action:** Led the shift of interviews from in person to virtual, restructuring the interview operation to work remotely while keeping candidate experience intact.
**Result:** Lowered process cost in line with the leadership directive without stalling recruiting.

### Project 3 — Phenom Outage Contingency

**Problem:** Phenom, the scheduling platform the program relied on, failed.
**Action:** Stood up a more manual scheduling approach to keep interviews and candidate flow moving during the outage.
**Result:** Recruiting continued through a platform failure rather than stalling, protecting the program timeline.

## TRANSITION & SUSTAINMENT DELIVERABLES

A deliberate part of closing the cycle was making sure the program did not walk out the door with me. I left the next team a playbook and a set of reusable artifacts so they could run the program from an established baseline instead of rebuilding it.

- **Recruiting Playbook:** Documented how the program runs end to end, from campus planning and career fair logistics through interviewing, offer management, and conversion
- **Reusable Artifacts:** Templates and materials accumulated across the cycle (job description and requirement definitions from the steering committee, swag and logistics processes, the volunteer interviewer model, and the Offeree Day format)
- **Contingency Reference:** The manual scheduling approach built during the Phenom outage, captured so the program has a fallback if the platform fails again
- **Virtual Interview Process:** The restructured remote interview operation documented for ongoing use

The point was sustainment. A program's benefit is only real if it survives a change in leadership, so handing over a playbook and working artifacts was how the next team could keep delivering hires without relearning everything from scratch.

## LESSONS LEARNED

1. A program lives or dies on coordination, not on any single event. The hiring numbers came from keeping 7 sites and their satellite teams pulling in the same direction across a full cycle, and the steering committee was what made that consistency possible.
2. The benefit is the point, and you protect it deliberately. Offers were not converting on their own, so Offeree Day existed specifically to move offer holders to yes. Naming that as its own initiative made the conversion problem something we worked rather than hoped on.
3. Resilience is part of the plan, not a reaction to it. When Phenom failed and when leadership pushed on cost, the program absorbed both by treating each as a contained change, a manual scheduling fallback in one case and a virtual interview transition in the other, rather than letting either derail the cycle.
4. A program should outlast the person running it. Capturing the work into a playbook and reusable artifacts meant the next team inherited a running program rather than a blank page, which is the difference between a one-time result and a benefit that sustains.

## PROGRAM NARRATIVE

I led the Technology Early Career Development Program's national recruiting effort across a full annual cycle. I set the vision for the program and drove its execution across 7 sites and a set of supporting satellite teams. The day-to-day of the program meant coordinating a lot of moving parts at once: scheduling career fair meetings, ordering and distributing swag, getting in front of students, and recruiting them into the TECDP pipeline. To keep role definitions consistent across all of that, I set up a steering committee that decided on job descriptions and requirements, and I took in community feedback and brought program members in as volunteers to conduct interviews with prospective candidates.

On top of running the ongoing operation, I spearheaded three new initiatives inside the program. The first was Offeree Day, which was like a sell day but built specifically for candidates who had already received offers, aimed at converting those offers into acceptances. The second was a shift of our interview process from in person to virtual, which I led in response to a leadership directive to reduce the cost of the process. The third was a contingency I had to stand up when Phenom, the platform we used for scheduling, failed, and I moved us to a more manual scheduling approach so recruiting did not stall.

The work took me to campuses across the country. I traveled to UT Austin in Texas, Washington University and George Washington University in St. Louis, UPenn in Philadelphia, and my home college of UConn, meeting students directly and representing the program. Over the course of this work I became a recognized brand ambassador for the program across the company.

By the end of the cycle the program had hired 65 full-time employees and 40 interns. Before the cycle closed, I made a point of capturing the program into a playbook and a set of reusable artifacts and handing them to the next team, so everything we had built, from the steering committee's role definitions to the swag logistics, the volunteer interviewer model, the Offeree Day format, and the manual scheduling fallback, was there for them to run from rather than rebuild. The thing that made it a program rather than a project was that it was never one effort with one endpoint. It was multiple teams across multiple sites running concurrent and recurring work toward a shared hiring outcome, with several discrete initiatives inside it that I owned from idea to result, and a handoff at the end designed so the benefit would continue after me.
